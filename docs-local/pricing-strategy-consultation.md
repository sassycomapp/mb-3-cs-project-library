## 1. Market Position — Do their clients own their data?

No. And this is the sharpest differentiator Mybizz has.

On Booksy, Vagaro, and Mindbody your client list is effectively platform property. When a client opens Booksy to book with you, they see other businesses offering similar services. Client loyalty shifts to the platform — your clients become "Booksy clients" rather than your clients. If you ever leave Booksy, those clients stay in the Booksy ecosystem.

Mindbody charges a 20% commission on a new client's first purchase via the app or marketplace, capped at $30 per transaction. You pay them to acquire a client who technically belongs to them.

On Mybizz every client record, every booking history, every contact, every email interaction lives in an isolated Anvil database that belongs exclusively to the subscriber. There is no shared infrastructure, no marketplace algorithm deciding your visibility, no competitor appearing on your listing page, and no platform taking a cut when a new client finds you.

Your instinct that $50 looks expensive at face value is wrong — and that is actually a messaging problem, not a pricing problem. Let me show you why.

A 3-person practice on Booksy pays roughly $70-90/month before processing fees, gets no real website, and does not own their client data. Mybizz at $50 all-in is cheaper and delivers more. The pricing is not the problem — the framing is.

The one genuine concern — your margin at $25
At $25/month with hosting costs of $180/client/year ($15/month), your margin on the first 50 clients is $10/month per client. That is very thin for a fully hosted, isolated application. At 50 beta clients your net from that cohort is $500/month — $6,000/year — before your own time, support, and development costs.
The $25 beta price is a strategic land-and-expand move, and that logic is sound. But be clear-eyed that the first 50 clients are essentially a subsidised beta programme, not a revenue base.

How to pitch the pricing
The TCO pitch:

"Most platforms advertise $25-30 a month. Add per-staff fees, processing commissions, marketplace cuts, add-ons, and the cost of your website sitting somewhere else — and you are paying $80-150 a month for less. Mybizz is $50. That is the number. Everything is included. Nothing is extra."

The ownership pitch:

"You are not renting a seat on someone else's platform. You are getting your own application — your website, your booking system, your client database — hosted and running, for $50 a month. Your competitors cannot see your clients. The platform cannot take a cut of your revenue. Your data does not disappear if you cancel."

The founding member pitch for the $25 tier:

"The first 50 businesses on Mybizz pay $25 a month, locked in for life. That is the founding member rate — in exchange for being early, giving feedback, and helping shape the product. After the first 50, the price is $50."


Should you change the pricing?
Three options worth considering:
Option A — Keep it as is, fix the pitch
The structure is sound. Just stop comparing to sticker prices and start comparing to total cost. $50 all-in wins that argument every time.
Option B — Raise the beta floor slightly
Move the founding member price from $25 to $35. Still a compelling discount, meaningfully better margin, and the TCO argument still holds against competitors. You would net $20/month per client after hosting instead of $10.
Option C — Add a one-time setup fee
Keep $25/$50 monthly but charge a setup fee of $150-200 for all clients (your premium onboarding is already $200 — make standard onboarding $150 and premium $300). This front-loads some revenue and filters out clients who are not serious.

My recommendation
Option B combined with Option C. Raise beta floor to $35, keep $50 for regular clients, charge a modest $150 setup fee for all clients. Your founding member story is still compelling, your margin is sustainable, and the setup fee signals that this is a real business platform, not a free trial.
The pricing direction is right. The pitch just needs to lead with what the competition actually costs when you add everything up.
